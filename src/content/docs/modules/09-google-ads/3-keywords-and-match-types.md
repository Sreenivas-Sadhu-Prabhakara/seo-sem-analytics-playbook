Two weeks into a campaign for Meena's Dental Clinic, you open the search-terms report and your stomach drops. Her ads have been showing for "dental assistant jobs Bangalore," "free dental camp," "dental college fees," and — inexplicably — "dentist salary." Each one a real click. Each one a rupee spent to reach someone who will never, ever book an appointment.

Nobody was reckless. This is just what happens when you pick keywords and walk away. Match types decide how *loosely* Google interprets your keywords, and negative keywords are the fence that keeps the wrong crowd out. Master both and you're spending on customers. Ignore them and you're subsidising job-seekers and freebie-hunters across the city.

## Why this pays

The gap between an amateur and a pro campaign often isn't the ad copy or the budget — it's whether someone is actively pruning the search terms. Negative-keyword discipline is boring, ongoing, and precisely the thing a busy owner will never do themselves. It's a huge chunk of what your monthly management fee is *for*. Show a client the "wasted spend, now blocked" list at the end of month one and you've justified the retainer on the spot.

## Match types: how loose is Google allowed to be?

A match type tells Google how far it can stray from your keyword when deciding whether to show your ad. Three exist today, from tightest to loosest.

| Match type | How you write it | What it triggers on | Reach vs precision |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exact** | `[dental implants]` | That search and very close variants (plurals, close synonyms, same intent) | Tightest, most precise |
| **Phrase** | `"dental implants"` | Searches that include the *meaning* of your phrase | Middle ground |
| **Broad** | `dental implants` (no punctuation) | Anything Google judges related — synonyms, related concepts, the whole neighbourhood of intent | Widest, least precise |

A few truths that trip people up:

- **Exact isn't literally exact anymore.** `[plumber near me]` can match "plumber nearby" or "local plumber" — same intent, different words. It's *close-variant* matching now, not character-for-character.
- **Phrase dropped the word-order rule.** Modern phrase match keys off *meaning*, not the literal sequence. `"movers Pune"` can match "affordable house movers in Pune."
- **Broad match has been rehabilitated — with a caveat.** Old broad match was notorious for showing your dentist ad on "tooth fairy costume." Today's broad match leans on Google's **smart bidding and audience signals** to interpret intent far better, and Google actively nudges you toward it. It *can* work well — but **only when it's fed conversion data to learn from and fenced with negatives.** On a brand-new account with no conversions, broad match is still a fast way to donate money.

:::note[🎓 Foundations]
Think of match types as how wide you cast a net. **Exact** is a spear — you hit the one fish you aimed at. **Phrase** is a small net — you catch that fish and its close cousins. **Broad** is a trawler — you catch everything in the water, including boots and bottles, and you'd better be able to throw the junk back (that's what negatives and conversion data do).
:::

## The starting strategy: start tight, then expand

For a new local campaign with thin data, begin **precise and cheap to learn**, then widen once you know what converts:

1. **Launch on phrase and exact** for your core, high-intent keywords. You'll get fewer impressions, but almost every click is relevant, and you burn little while learning.
2. **Mine the search-terms report** (below) every few days. Add good new searches as keywords; add junk as negatives.
3. **Only once conversions are tracking** and you know your winners, *cautiously* test broad match on your best-performing keyword — with smart bidding on and your negative list already built. Broad match is a scaling tool, not a starting tool.

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
**Broad match + no negatives + no conversion tracking = a budget bonfire.** This is the classic beginner blowup. Google will happily spend your client's full daily budget on tangential searches because it has no conversion signal telling it what "good" looks like, and no negatives telling it what to avoid. If you're going to touch broad match, earn the right to first: conversions tracked, negatives loaded, smart bidding running. Otherwise, stay on phrase and exact.
:::

## The search-terms report: your goldmine and your fence

This is the most important report in the account, and most amateurs never open it. **Keywords** are what you *bid on*; **search terms** are what people *actually typed* to trigger your ad. The report shows the gap.

> 📸 *[Screenshot placeholder: Google Ads → Campaign → Insights & reports → Search terms, showing actual queries, cost, and conversions per term. Menus move — if it's not here, search the account for "search terms."]*

Work it in two directions:

- **Mine negatives.** Any search that's clearly irrelevant — a job-seeker, a student, a freebie-hunter, another city — becomes a negative keyword so it never costs you again.
- **Mine new keywords.** Any search that converted and *isn't* already a keyword gets promoted into the right ad group (often as exact match, so you control it tightly).

Do this weekly at minimum in the early days. The report is the engine of the whole campaign getting cheaper and sharper over time.

## Negative keywords: the discipline that pays the rent

A **negative keyword** tells Google "never show my ad for searches containing this word." It's the highest-leverage housekeeping in paid search. Categories of negatives to build from day one:

- **Freebie intent:** `free`, `gratis`, `no cost`, `discount camp`
- **Job/career intent:** `jobs`, `vacancy`, `salary`, `career`, `internship`, `hiring`
- **Research/DIY intent:** `how to`, `diy`, `youtube`, `meaning`, `pdf`, `course`
- **Wrong price bracket:** `cheap`, `cheapest` (if the client is premium)
- **Wrong locations:** every city and area the client does *not* serve
- **Wrong product:** adjacent things you don't offer (a dentist blocking `denture repair kit`)

You can apply negatives at the ad-group level (precise) or campaign level (broad housekeeping), and you can save a reusable **negative keyword list** to apply across campaigns and clients.

:::tip[⚔️ War story]
A freelancer (composite) ran a broad-match campaign for a premium salon and forgot negatives. Within four days, half the budget had gone to "beautician job vacancy," "beauty course fees," and "cheap haircut" — none of them a paying client for a ₹2,000 balayage. The fix took ten minutes: a negative list blocking `job`, `course`, `cheap`, and neighbouring towns. Same budget, and enquiries appeared the following week. The money wasn't the problem; the missing fence was.
:::

:::tip[🚀 Level up]
Watch for **negatives that fight your keywords.** If you add `implant` as a negative to block "implant jobs," you've just blocked your own "dental implants" keyword too — negatives out-muscle keywords. Block the *specific* junk phrase (`implant jobs`, as a phrase negative) rather than the useful word. And re-check your negative list quarterly; a word you blocked last season might be a service the client added since.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Setting keywords once and never opening the search-terms report.
- Launching broad match on a fresh account with no conversions and no negatives.
- No negative list at all, so freebie- and job-seekers eat the budget.
- Over-blocking with a broad negative that also kills your real keywords.
- Forgetting to add *locations you don't serve* as negatives on a geo-targeted campaign.

## Do this now

For your capstone client, write **6–10 tight, high-intent keywords** in phrase and exact match, grouped into the ad groups you sketched last lesson. Then write **at least 15 negative keywords** — start from the swipe list above and add client-specific junk (wrong services, neighbouring areas, price mismatches). This negative list is a deliverable in the module Assignment, so keep it.

<details>
<summary>Q: Your client's ad keeps showing for "[service] jobs" and "[service] salary." What's the fix, and why not just pause the keyword?</summary>

Add `jobs` and `salary` as negative keywords (phrase or campaign-level), not pause the keyword — the keyword itself is fine and profitable; it's specific *searches* that are junk. Negatives surgically block the bad queries while keeping the good ones flowing.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q: Why is broad match safer to use in month three than on launch day?</summary>

Because by month three the account has conversion data for smart bidding to learn from and a negative-keyword list built from the search-terms report. Broad match relies on those signals to interpret intent well; without them, on a fresh account, it just spends widely and blindly.
</details>
