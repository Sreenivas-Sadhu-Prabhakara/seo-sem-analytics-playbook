Meena hands you the number every small-clinic owner eventually says out loud: "Let's start with ₹10,000 a month and see." That's roughly ₹330 a day. It is not a lot. Spread it across a whole city, twelve keywords, and every hour of the day, and it evaporates into a puff of impressions and nothing to show. Concentrate that same ₹330 like a laser, and it can produce a genuinely useful stream of enquiries.

Small budgets don't fail because they're small. They fail because they're *diffuse*. This lesson is about focus — where the money goes, and how you tell Google to bid it.

## Why this pays

Any agency can spend ₹5 lakh a month and get results — the volume hides the waste. Making a *small* budget work is the harder, rarer skill, and it's exactly what local SMBs need. Prove you can turn ₹10,000 into measurable leads and you'll never lack for clients, because that's the budget most of India's small businesses actually have. Discipline with a small budget is your differentiator.

## Daily budget vs the monthly reality

Google Ads runs on a **daily budget** set per campaign, with one nuance you must tell the client so they don't panic:

- Google can spend **up to about 2× your daily budget on a busy day** (less on a quiet one), aiming to keep your *monthly* total near **daily × 30.4**. A ₹330/day campaign should land around ₹10,000/month even if a day swings to ₹600.
- **Set the daily number from the monthly budget:** `monthly ÷ 30.4`. ₹10,000/month → about ₹330/day.
- **The client's real cap is monthly.** Watch month-to-date spend; don't let one high-traffic day spook anyone — it evens out.

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
Warn the client *before* launch about the up-to-2×-per-day behaviour, in writing. Owners who see "₹640 spent" on a ₹330/day budget assume you've made an error or been hacked. One calm sentence in your setup email — "some days run higher, some lower; the month stays on target" — prevents a frantic 9pm phone call. Manage the expectation and it's a non-event.
:::

## Concentrate the small budget: the four levers

A modest budget wins by being narrow on purpose. Pull all four levers:

1. **Fewer keywords.** Back your 5–8 highest-intent keywords, not thirty maybes. Depth beats breadth — you want enough spend per keyword to actually learn.
2. **Tight geography.** Don't advertise across Bengaluru if the clinic serves Jayanagar and two adjacent areas. Every rupee spent on a distant searcher who won't travel is wasted.
3. **The right hours (ad scheduling).** If enquiries are answered 9am–8pm, consider concentrating spend there — a click at 3am that rings an unanswered phone is a donation. (Restrict hours only once data shows when conversions happen; don't guess on day one.)
4. **The right devices and audiences, once you know them.** If mobile converts far better later, lean into it. Early on, keep it simple.

The mantra: **a small budget must be deep, not wide.** One tight ad group, one small area, your best keywords, running when someone can answer — that's a campaign that learns and produces. Thirty keywords across a metro at ₹330/day does neither.

:::note[💰 Rupee check]
*Illustrative only — CPCs are set by a live auction and vary enormously by niche, city, and season. Verify current numbers with Keyword Planner before you quote.* A realistic **small local Search campaign** often runs in the region of **₹300–₹600/day** (~₹9,000–₹18,000/month). Cost-per-click ranges from a few rupees for low-competition local services to **₹60–₹100+** for cutthroat categories (loans, insurance, "packers and movers," some legal and medical terms). So ₹330/day might buy anywhere from ~5 clicks (expensive niche) to 50+ clicks (cheap niche) a day. **Never promise a lead count.** Quote a range, explain the auction sets the price, and let the first two weeks of real data replace the estimate.
:::

## Bidding strategies: telling Google how to spend each rupee

Your **bid strategy** is the instruction for how Google bids in each auction. The menu, simplified:

| Strategy | What Google optimises for | Needs conversion data? | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Manual CPC** | Nothing automatic — you set max bids by keyword | No | Total control; tiny/simple accounts |
| **Maximize Clicks** | The most clicks for your budget | No | **A brand-new campaign with no conversion history** |
| **Maximize Conversions** | The most conversions for your budget | **Yes** | Once conversions are tracking and flowing |
| **Target CPA** | Conversions at a target cost each | **Yes — needs history** | Mature campaigns with steady conversions |
| **Target ROAS** | Revenue return per rupee spent | **Yes — needs revenue data** | E-commerce with tracked sale values |

:::note[🎓 Foundations]
"Smart bidding" is the umbrella term for the strategies where **Google's machine learning sets each bid in real time** — Maximize Conversions, Target CPA, Target ROAS. It's genuinely powerful, but it runs on fuel: **conversion data.** With no conversions to learn from, the machine has nothing to optimise toward and will spend oddly. Smart bidding on an empty tank isn't smart — it's guessing with your client's money.
:::

## The thin-data starting sequence

Here's the order that keeps a new campaign out of trouble:

1. **Launch on Maximize Clicks** (or Manual CPC if you want tight control). With zero conversion history, this simply buys relevant traffic efficiently while you gather data. **Set a max-CPC bid limit** so a single pricey keyword can't run away with the budget.
2. **Let conversions accumulate.** Keep the campaign running, prune with the search-terms report, and wait until you have a *meaningful* number of tracked conversions — a rough rule of thumb is **at least ~15–30 conversions in ~30 days** before smart bidding has enough to learn from. (There's no magic number; more is better.)
3. **Then switch to Maximize Conversions.** Now the machine has fuel. Let it re-learn for a week or two before you judge it — bid strategies wobble right after a change.
4. **Only later, layer on Target CPA** once conversions are steady and you know your typical cost per lead. Set the target near your actual recent CPA, not a fantasy number, or Google will throttle the campaign chasing an impossible goal.

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
**Switching to smart bidding with zero conversions tracked is the most expensive beginner mistake in this module.** With nothing to optimise toward, Maximize Conversions or Target CPA will spend erratically — chasing a signal that doesn't exist — and you'll have no idea why the numbers look strange. The rule is iron: **conversion tracking working → conversions actually flowing → then smart bidding.** Not before. If tracking isn't live yet (we build it in [lesson 6](/seo-sem-analytics-playbook/modules/09-google-ads/6-landing-pages-and-conversion/)), stay on Maximize Clicks.
:::

## The metrics that matter (and the vanity ones that don't)

Watch these weekly, and teach the client to look at the right ones:

- **Conversions** and **cost per conversion (CPA)** — the point of the whole exercise. This is what you report.
- **Conversion rate** — clicks that became leads. Low? Suspect the landing page, not the ads.
- **Cost per click (CPC)** — creeping up? Check Quality Score and competition.
- **Click-through rate (CTR)** — an ad-quality signal; low CTR often means weak copy or poor match.
- **Impression share** *(context, not a target)* — how often you showed when you were eligible.

**Impressions and clicks alone are vanity metrics.** A client dazzled by "50,000 impressions!" is being distracted from the only question that matters: how many enquiries, at what cost each? Anchor every conversation on CPA and conversions.

:::tip[⚔️ War story]
A freelancer (composite) inherited a ₹15,000/month campaign that had been on Target CPA since launch — set by someone who never wired conversion tracking. With no conversions to see, Google had quietly throttled it to a trickle of impressions, "protecting" a target it could never measure: two weeks of near-zero traffic, full budget "available," client baffled. The fix — switch to Maximize Clicks, fix tracking, gather data for a month, *then* return to smart bidding — brought traffic and leads back. Match the bid strategy to the data you actually have, not the data you wish you had.
:::

## Scaling: pour more only where it converts

Once data arrives, growth is boring and disciplined:

- **Find the winners.** Which keywords and ad groups produce conversions at an acceptable CPA?
- **Feed them.** Raise budget or bids on the converters; expand their keywords (mined from the search-terms report).
- **Starve the losers.** Pause or cut keywords that spend without converting. Every rupee moved from a loser to a winner improves the whole account.
- **Change one thing at a time.** If you raise the budget, swap the bid strategy, *and* rewrite the ads all at once, you'll never know which move worked. Scale in single, measured steps.

:::tip[🚀 Level up]
When a campaign is genuinely limited by budget (Google flags "Limited by budget"), don't reflexively raise it. First check whether you're *wasting* budget on non-converting search terms — tightening negatives often frees enough spend to feed the winners without asking the client for more money. Efficiency is free growth, and clients love the freelancer who makes the *same* budget do more before asking for a bigger one.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Spreading a small budget across too many keywords, too wide a geo, all hours.
- Panicking at the up-to-2×-daily overspend instead of watching the monthly total.
- Switching to smart bidding before conversions are tracked and flowing.
- Setting a Target CPA far below reality, so Google throttles the campaign.
- Judging a bid-strategy change after two days instead of letting it re-learn.
- Reporting impressions and clicks instead of conversions and cost per conversion.

## Do this now

For your capstone client, decide and write down: (1) the **daily budget** from their monthly figure (`monthly ÷ 30.4`), (2) the **geo** (specific localities or a small radius, not the whole city), (3) the **starting bid strategy** (Maximize Clicks with a max-CPC cap, unless conversions are already flowing), and (4) a note on **ad scheduling** if enquiries are only answered certain hours.
