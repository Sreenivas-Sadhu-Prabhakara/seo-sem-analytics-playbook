You search the client's business name. There they are — number one, glowing, five stars. "See," the owner beams, "we're number one on Google." And they are... for the seven people a month who already know the clinic's name and just want the phone number.

Now search "dentist near me" from a phone two kilometres away. They've vanished. Three competitors and a JustDial listing sit where the customers actually look. *That's* the real audit. Ranking for your own name is like winning a race you're the only one running. This lesson teaches you to measure the visibility that brings **strangers**, not the loyalists who already know the name.

## Why this pays

Organic and local search is the highest-margin channel a local SMB has — no cost-per-click, no ad budget, just the compounding value of showing up when someone nearby needs you. Diagnosing *why* they don't show up (weak GBP? no service pages? competitors with 200 reviews?) is exactly what they'll pay you to fix over the next several months. Every gap you find here is a line item in the recommended plan.

## Start where the customers are: the Google Business Profile

For a local business, the **Google Business Profile (GBP)** — the listing with the map pin, reviews and photos — often matters more than the website. It's the first thing a "near me" searcher sees. Grade it hard:

- **Claimed and verified?** An unclaimed listing is a house someone else can walk into. If it's unverified, that's finding number one.
- **Primary category correct?** "Dental clinic" vs "dentist" vs "orthodontist" changes which searches you appear for. Wrong or vague categories quietly kill visibility.
- **NAP consistency.** Name, Address, Phone — do they match *exactly* across the website, GBP, JustDial and Facebook? Google trusts consistency; "Shop 4" vs "Shop No. 4" vs "#4" is three different addresses to a machine.
- **Reviews.** How many, what average, and — crucially — how *recent*? A business with 12 reviews (last one 2022) loses to a rival with 180 and one from last Tuesday. Are they even replying to reviews?
- **Photos.** Real, recent, plentiful? Or three blurry shots from 2019?
- **Posts, hours, services, Q&A.** Are they using GBP's free tools at all, or treating it like a phone-book entry?

:::note[🎓 Foundations]
**Why "near me" is the whole game.** When someone types "physiotherapy near me," Google doesn't just match words — it factors in *distance*, *relevance* (categories, keywords) and *prominence* (reviews, links, mentions). This local ranking is separate from classic organic "blue link" ranking, which is why a business can rank on page one for a blog post yet be invisible on the map. Local SEO gets a whole module (Module 5); here you're just diagnosing the current state.

## Do they rank for their money keywords — and "near me"?

Make a short list of **money keywords** — the searches a ready-to-buy customer types. For a Jayanagar dental clinic (composite example): "dentist jayanagar," "root canal bangalore," "teeth whitening near me," "dental implants cost bangalore." Then check where they actually land.

- Search each on a **phone**, ideally from near the business, in an **incognito/private window** so your own history doesn't personalise the results.
- Note position in three places: the **Map Pack** (the top 3 map results), the **organic** links below it, and whether a competitor's **ad** sits above everything.
- Include Hinglish and vernacular variants where real customers use them — many search "dentist ke paas" or in the local script. Don't assume everyone types textbook English.

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
**Confusing brand-name ranking with real visibility.** Ranking #1 for your own business name is the default — Google would have to actively hate you to fail at that. It proves nothing about whether *new* customers can find you. Always test the **non-brand, intent-rich** searches ("gym in Kukatpally," "CA for GST filing Pune"). If the owner shows you a #1 brand ranking as proof of "good SEO," gently redirect: "That's people who already know you. Let's see what a stranger sees." That reframe alone often sells the whole engagement.
:::

## On-page basics: the shop with no name board

Now the website's own SEO. You're not doing a full on-page teardown (that's Module 6) — you're checking whether the fundamentals exist:

- **Title tags.** Does each page have a unique, descriptive `<title>`? A page titled "Home" or "Untitled" is a shop with no name board. View the browser tab, or the source, to check.
- **Headings.** Is there one clear `<h1>` per page that says what the page is about, in human language a customer would search?
- **Service pages.** This is the big one for SMBs. Does every service they sell have its **own dedicated page** — "Root Canal Treatment," "Teeth Whitening," "Dental Implants" — or is everything crammed into one thin "Services" page and a phone number? Google can't rank a page that doesn't exist. Missing service pages are the most common, most fixable SEO gap you'll find.
- **Thin content.** Are pages a paragraph of fluff, or do they actually answer what a customer wants to know (what it costs, what to expect, why choose them)?
- **Local signals.** Is the city/area, address and a map on the site? Does the content mention the neighbourhoods they serve?

## Content gaps: what customers search vs what exists

Line up two lists. On the left, what customers actually search — the questions, the "near me"s, the "cost of X in [city]"s. On the right, the pages the site actually has. The empty rows on the right are your **content gaps** — the pages that should exist and don't. "How much does a root canal cost in Bangalore?" is a question thousands search and few local clinics answer honestly on a page. That gap is a ranking opportunity with your name on it.

## The competitor comparison

Pick the two or three competitors the owner named in discovery (plus whoever actually ranks for the money keywords — sometimes not the same list). Put them side by side:

| Signal | Your client | Competitor A | Competitor B |
|---|---|---|---|
| GBP reviews (count / avg) | 14 / 4.6 | 180 / 4.7 | 96 / 4.4 |
| Recent review (last 30 days)? | No | Yes | Yes |
| Ranks in Map Pack for money kw? | No | Yes | Sometimes |
| Dedicated service pages | 1 (blob) | 12 | 6 |
| Site blog / FAQ content | None | Active | Some |

*(Figures above are an illustrative composite, not a real business.)*

This table is persuasion gold. "Your rival has 180 reviews and answers every one; you have 14 and answer none" lands harder than any lecture — you're not shaming, you're showing a gap and, implicitly, the way to close it.

:::note[📋 Swipe this]
**The SEO-audit scorecard — score each 0–2 (0 = missing, 1 = weak, 2 = solid):**

- [ ] GBP claimed, verified, correct primary category
- [ ] NAP consistent across web, GBP, directories
- [ ] Review volume & recency competitive vs rivals
- [ ] Replies to reviews (yes/no)
- [ ] Ranks in Map Pack for ≥1 money keyword
- [ ] Ranks organically (non-brand) for ≥1 money keyword
- [ ] Unique title tag on every key page
- [ ] Dedicated page per service sold
- [ ] Location/area signals present on site
- [ ] Content answers real customer questions (cost, process, FAQs)

Total /20 gives you an at-a-glance "SEO health" number for the report — and a satisfying "before" score to beat later.
:::

Local SEO retainers for a single-location SMB sit — illustratively — somewhere around ₹10,000–₹40,000 a month depending on competition, city and scope, often with a one-time GBP-and-on-page setup fee on top. These are ballpark ranges to calibrate your thinking, not a rate card; a hyper-competitive market (real estate in Gurugram) commands far more than a village-town boutique. Verify current local rates before you quote.

## Common mistakes

- **Auditing rankings while logged in / on your usual browser.** Personalised, localised results lie. Use incognito and note your location.
- **Only checking English keywords.** Hinglish and vernacular searches are huge in India.
- **Treating GBP as an afterthought.** For local, it's often the main event.
- **Listing gaps without ranking them.** Every finding needs a rough "how big / how hard" so it can be prioritised later.

## Do this now

Build the competitor comparison table for your capstone client versus their two scariest rivals, and run the SEO scorecard. Write down the single biggest visibility gap — the one that, closed, would bring the most new customers. That sentence becomes a headline finding in your report.

<details>
<summary>Q: A client proudly ranks #1 for their business name. Why is that weak evidence of good SEO?</summary>

Because branded search is people who already know the business and just want the number — you'd have to be almost trying to fail. Real SEO value is showing up for *non-brand, intent-rich* searches ("dentist near me," "root canal cost [city]") where new customers actually look. Test those instead.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q: The site has one "Services" page listing eight services. What's the SEO problem and the fix?</summary>

Google ranks pages, not sentences. Eight services crammed onto one thin page can't each rank for their specific search. The fix is a dedicated, genuinely useful page per service — a common, high-impact opportunity to put in the report.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q: Why include Hinglish and vernacular keyword variants in an SEO audit?</summary>

Because a large share of Indian searchers don't type textbook English — they search in Hinglish or their local language and script. Auditing only clean English keywords undercounts the real demand and misses ranking opportunities competitors may already be capturing.
</details>
