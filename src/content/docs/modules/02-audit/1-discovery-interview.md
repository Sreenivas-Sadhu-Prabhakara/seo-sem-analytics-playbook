Your first real meeting with the client. You've got a notebook, a fresh cup of chai, and a powerful urge to prove you know your stuff. So you launch in: "Your bounce rate is high and your Core Web Vitals are failing and we should really—"

Stop. You've just told a woman who has run a successful dental clinic for eleven years that you understand her business better than she does, in the first ninety seconds. You don't. Not yet. The single most valuable thing you can do in this meeting is **shut up and ask good questions.**

## Why this pays

Everything you'll audit, recommend and charge for hangs off this conversation. Get the goals wrong and you'll spend three months optimising for traffic when the client only cares about phone calls. Get them right and every later recommendation lands, because you can say "you told me appointments were the goal — here's how we get more of them." Discovery is where you stop being a vendor and start being a partner. It's also, quietly, where you learn how much this client is worth to you.

## Ask what they actually want (not what you assume they want)

Owners rarely open with "I want more organic sessions." They say "phone's gone quiet" or "the new clinic across the road is eating my lunch." Your job is to translate the worry into a goal, and the goal into a number.

Walk through these, in roughly this order:

1. **Goals.** "If we're sitting here a year from now and you're thrilled, what changed?" Let them talk.
2. **Best and most-profitable customers.** Not all revenue is equal. A dental clinic's whitening and implant patients might be 10x the margin of a routine cleaning. Which customers do they *want more of*?
3. **Seasonality.** "When's your busy season? Your dead months?" A tax consultant is slammed in July and asleep in November. A wedding photographer lives and dies by the muhurtham calendar.
4. **Current lead sources.** "Where do customers come from today — walk-ins, JustDial, WhatsApp forwards, referrals, that one hoarding near the signal?" You're mapping what already works.
5. **What 'success' looks like.** Push for a metric. "More enquiries" → "how many a week now, and how many would feel like a win?"
6. **Margins and capacity.** Can they even *handle* more business? A salon with two chairs can't absorb 200 new bookings a month. Knowing capacity stops you from overselling.
7. **Past marketing burns.** "What have you tried that didn't work?" This is gold — it tells you what they're sceptical of and where a previous freelancer or agency let them down.
8. **Competitors they fear.** "Who do you lose customers to?" Name names. You'll audit these people later.

:::note[📋 Swipe this]
**The discovery question list — steal it verbatim:**

- "Walk me through your business in your own words — pretend I know nothing."
- "Who's your best type of customer, and what are they worth to you over a year?"
- "How do most customers find you *today*?"
- "When someone rings up, what do they usually ask for?"
- "Busy months? Slow months?"
- "What have you already tried to get more customers — and how did it go?"
- "Which competitors worry you, and why?"
- "If you could wave a wand and fix one thing about how you get customers, what would it be?"
- "How would we *both* know, in 90 days, that hiring me was a good decision?"

Send this as an agenda before the call. It makes you look organised and gets them thinking.
:::

## Listen for the ONE metric that matters

Somewhere in that conversation, one number is quietly running the whole business. For most Indian local SMBs it is **not** website traffic. It's **calls, WhatsApp enquiries, walk-ins, or bookings** — the moment a stranger becomes a lead.

A physiotherapist doesn't care about impressions; she cares about new-patient calls. A restaurant cares about table reservations and Swiggy/Zomato orders. A coaching centre cares about demo-class sign-ups. Find that metric and write it at the top of your notes in capital letters, because your entire audit — and every later report — should orbit it. Traffic that doesn't move the ONE metric is a vanity number, and you'll learn to say so out loud.

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
**Assuming you know their business better than they do.** You've audited forty websites; you have *not* run their business for a decade. When the owner says "actually, most of our profit comes from corporate annual dental check-up contracts, not walk-ins," believe them and rebuild your assumptions on the spot. The fastest way to lose a client's trust is to nod politely and then optimise for the thing *you* find interesting instead of the thing that pays *their* rent.
:::

## Capture the boring-but-critical stuff: assets and access

Before the meeting ends, you need two lists. Skip this and you'll spend the next fortnight chasing passwords over WhatsApp.

**Brand assets:**

- Logo files, brand colours, tagline
- Real photos (interiors, staff, work, products — you'll need these for GBP and the site)
- Existing service/price lists, offers, USPs
- Testimonials or review screenshots they're proud of

**Access you'll need:**

| What | Why you need it | How to get it cleanly |
|---|---|---|
| Google Business Profile | The heart of local SEO | Ask them to add you as a **Manager**, not Owner |
| Website admin / hosting | To make on-page changes | Editor/admin login, or via their web person |
| Google Ads account | To audit and run ads | Request access via your Ads manager account (MCC) |
| GA4 & Search Console | To measure anything | Add your email with the right role |
| Domain registrar | Occasionally, for DNS | Only when needed |

:::note[💰 Rupee check]
Discovery is billable, and framing it well protects your rate. Some freelancers roll discovery + audit into a single paid **audit engagement** — illustratively in the ₹8,000–₹25,000 range for a small local business, depending on scope and city — which then credits toward a retainer if the client continues. Treat these as ballpark figures, not fixed rates; the market moves, and a Mumbai boutique agency and a solo freelancer in Coimbatore price very differently. The point isn't the number — it's that a *diagnosis has value* and shouldn't be given away to look eager.
:::

:::tip[⚔️ War story]
A freelancer we'll call Arjun (composite) skipped discovery and jumped straight to "SEO." He spent a month ranking a Jaipur boutique for "designer lehenga Jaipur." Great keyword. Traffic climbed. The owner fired him anyway — because 80% of her actual revenue came from repeat bridal clients booking full trousseaus by referral, and she needed a *booking system and WhatsApp catalogue*, not cold search traffic. He optimised beautifully for a goal she never had. One good discovery question — "where does your profit actually come from?" — would have saved the gig.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Talking more than the owner.** If you're not the one listening, you're doing it wrong.
- **Not writing it down.** Memory is not a discovery tool. Type notes live or record (with permission).
- **Forgetting access.** Ending discovery without a plan to get GBP/website/ads access means a week of dead time.
- **Accepting "more customers" as a goal.** Push every fuzzy goal down to a number and a metric.

## Do this now

Book or role-play a 45-minute discovery call with your capstone client. Use the Swipe-this list. By the end, you must be able to write three things on one line: **(1)** their ONE metric, **(2)** their most-profitable customer type, and **(3)** the competitor they fear most. If you can't, you didn't dig deep enough — go back.

<details>
<summary>Q: A client says their goal is "more website traffic." Why should you push back?</summary>

Because traffic is almost never the real goal for a local SMB — it's a proxy. Dig until you find the business outcome underneath: calls, bookings, walk-ins or enquiries. If you optimise for traffic that doesn't produce those, you'll deliver rising numbers and a fired-freelancer email in the same quarter.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q: Why ask about a client's most-profitable customer, not just their most common one?</summary>

Because your job is to grow *profit*, not volume. Chasing the high-frequency, low-margin customer can flood a business with work that barely pays. Knowing the high-margin customer tells you which keywords, services and campaigns are actually worth fighting for.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q: You end discovery without arranging any account access. What's the practical consequence?</summary>

You've bought yourself a week (or three) of dead time chasing logins over WhatsApp before you can audit or change anything. Access is part of discovery, not an afterthought — leave the meeting with a clear plan for GBP, website, ads and analytics access.
</details>
