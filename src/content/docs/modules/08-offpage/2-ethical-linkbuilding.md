The client asks the question every SMB asks once they understand links are votes: "Okay, so how do we *get* the good ones?"

Here's the reframe that unlocks the whole answer. A local business already sits inside a web of real relationships — suppliers, partners, the school down the road, the running club, the trade association, the local paper. Most of those relationships already exist offline. Ethical link-building is mostly just **asking the internet to reflect relationships that are already real.** You're not manufacturing authority. You're documenting it.

## Why this pays

This is the retainer engine. On-page you fix once; this you do every month, and it compounds. It's also the work that's genuinely safe — every link here would survive a Google engineer reading it over your shoulder. And it does double duty: a sponsorship that earns a link often earns *customers* too, which makes the client feel the ROI in their till, not just a rankings chart.

## The one test that governs everything

Before you chase any link, run it through the filter from the last lesson:

> **Would this link exist if Google didn't?**

If a real human would put it there because it genuinely helps their audience or reflects a real relationship — go for it. If it only exists to move rankings — walk away. Print this above your desk. It'll keep you and your clients out of trouble for your entire career.

## Where a local business's links actually live

You don't need a fancy prospecting tool for most of this. You need to think like the business's neighbour.

### 1. Suppliers, partners and the "we work with" web
Every business buys from someone and works alongside someone. Equipment brands, wholesalers, franchisors, software vendors and B2B partners often have a "dealers," "partners," "stockists" or "find a clinic" page. Ask to be listed. For Sunrise Physiotherapy, that's the rehab-equipment brand they buy from, the local gyms they take referrals from, and the orthopaedic surgeons they work with.

### 2. Local sponsorships — events, schools, teams
Sponsor the neighbourhood 10K run, a school sports day, a local cultural festival, a Durga Puja pandal, a colony cricket team. Organisers almost always list and link their sponsors. This is the single most reliable local-link play *and* it puts the brand in front of exactly the right neighbourhood.

### 3. Chambers of commerce & industry associations
Local business chambers, trade bodies and professional associations (a physiotherapy council, a restaurant association, a jewellers' association) usually have member directories with links. Membership is often modest and buys you a credible, relevant, local link plus real networking.

### 4. Local press and bloggers
The neighbourhood news portal, the city lifestyle blog, the "best cafés in Koramangala" listicle writer, the mommy-blogger who covers local kids' activities. A genuine story, a free camp, a new-in-town angle — covered by a real local outlet — is a superb link. (We go deep on earning press in the next lesson.)

### 5. Genuinely useful resource pages
Some sites keep curated lists: "sports-injury resources for Bengaluru runners," "vendors for your Bangalore wedding." If your client genuinely belongs on that list and adds value to it, a polite ask often works — *because you actually deserve to be there.*

### 6. Being a source / expert quotes
Journalists and bloggers constantly need a quick expert quote. A physio commenting on marathon-season injuries, a CA commenting on a tax-filing deadline, a boutique owner on festive fashion trends. Get quoted, get a link and a credibility bump. (More on this and the reporter-query services in the next lesson.)

## The outreach that actually works

You'll get the full email templates next lesson, but the principle for local link-building is simpler than digital PR: **lead with the real relationship.** "We're a customer of yours and noticed you list partners — could you add us?" converts far better than any clever pitch, because it's true and low-effort for them to say yes.

:::note[💰 Rupee check]
*Illustrative — verify current rates, the market moves.* Local sponsorships often run anywhere from a few thousand rupees for a colony team or school event up to larger sums for a well-known city run. Chamber/association memberships are frequently in the low-thousands per year. You can package ongoing off-page work into a retainer — many freelancers bundle link + reputation work into a monthly fee rather than billing per link. Never sell "we'll get you N links for ₹X"; that framing pushes you toward buying junk to hit a number.
:::

:::tip[⚔️ War story]
A small Coimbatore dental clinic (composite) sponsored a local school's annual sports day for a modest sum — mostly to be a good neighbour. The school's site listed them as a health partner with a link, the local paper's event coverage named them, and three parents booked appointments that month after seeing the banner. One ethical move produced a relevant local link, a press mention *and* paying patients. That's the whole thesis of this module in one afternoon.
:::

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
Don't confuse "local directory" with "directory spam." A relevant, real, curated local listing (a genuine city business directory, JustDial, your association's member page) is fine. Blasting the same listing into 300 generic global "SEO directories" is the spam pattern from the last lesson. Relevance and realness are the dividing line — quality over a submission count, always.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Chasing links from big irrelevant sites while ignoring the small relevant local ones that move the needle.
- Sponsoring things purely for the link — organisers can smell it, and it fails the "would this exist anyway" test. Sponsor things the client actually cares about.
- Forgetting to *ask*. Half these links never appear simply because nobody requested the listing they'd have happily added.

<details>
<summary>Q: A client wants to sponsor a random national blog's "top 100 businesses" post for a link. Good idea?</summary>

No — that's a paid placement with no real relevance or relationship; it fails the "would this exist anyway?" test and, if it passes link credit, it violates Google's paid-link policy. Redirect that budget to a *local, relevant* sponsorship they actually care about, where the link is a genuine by-product.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q: Why prioritise a small neighbourhood blog over a huge unrelated high-authority site?</summary>

Because relevance and locality are what count for an SMB. A Bengaluru health blog linking a Bengaluru physio is a topical, local, editorial vote that also reaches real potential customers. A giant unrelated site sends a weaker, off-topic signal and no relevant audience.
</details>
