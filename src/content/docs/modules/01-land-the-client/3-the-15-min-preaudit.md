There are two kinds of freelancer who walk into a business. The first says, "Hi, I do digital marketing, would you be interested?" and gets shown the door before the sentence ends. The second says, "I noticed something about how your clinic shows up on Google — mind if I show you? It's costing you patients." The owner leans in.

The difference between them is fifteen minutes of homework.

This lesson is that homework: a fast, repeatable **pre-audit** you run *before* any contact, on any prospect, using only your phone and a browser. It gives you the one thing that makes cold outreach work — something specific, true, and slightly alarming to say.

## Why a free insight beats a sales pitch

Nobody wants to be *sold to.* Everybody wants to be *told something useful about their own business.* When you lead with a specific, verifiable observation — "you don't appear for 'gym near me' in your own area, but three competitors do" — three things happen at once: you prove you did your homework, you demonstrate competence without a slide deck, and you hand them a problem their brain can't un-see. The pitch sells itself after that.

So you never open with "hire me." You open with "here's what I noticed, and here's roughly what it's costing you." Free value first. Always.

## The 15-minute pre-audit checklist

Set a timer. You are not doing a deep technical audit here (that's later, and it's paid). You're gathering *pitch ammunition.* Run these on your prospect and one or two of their competitors:

**Google Business Profile (5 min)**
1. Search the business name. Is the profile **claimed and complete** — hours, phone, address, category correct?
2. **Reviews:** how many, what rating, and has the owner *replied* to any (especially the bad ones)?
3. **Photos:** are there recent, decent photos, or three dark blurs from 2020?
4. **Category:** is it right? A physio listed as generic "doctor" is quietly invisible for the searches that matter.

**Local search visibility (4 min)**
5. Search the **"service + area"** term a real customer would use — "dentist in Indiranagar," "spoken English classes Kukatpally." Where does the prospect land? Are they in the **map pack** (the top 3 with the little map), on page one, or nowhere?
6. Who *is* winning those searches? Note the top competitor and one reason they're winning (more reviews, better profile, an actual website).

**Website & mobile (4 min)**
7. Do they have a **real website**, a dead link, or nothing? Open it **on your phone** — does it load fast, look decent, and show a phone number / WhatsApp / directions *without pinching and zooming?*
8. Is there an obvious way to **contact or book** (click-to-call, WhatsApp, enquiry form), or does the customer have to work for it?

**Advertising & competition (2 min)**
9. When you search their main term, are **competitors running Google Ads** (look for the "Sponsored" label) while your prospect isn't? That's a live customer stream the prospect is watching flow to rivals.

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha — search "incognito," and mind your location]
Google personalises results. If you've visited the prospect's site five times, Google may flatter them in *your* results while real customers never see them. Check rankings in an **incognito/private window**, and remember Maps results depend on the *searcher's* location — so judge visibility as a customer standing in that neighbourhood would experience it, not from across town. Report what a real local customer sees.
:::

:::note[🎓 Foundations]
The **map pack** (or "local pack") is the boxed set of usually three businesses with a map that Google shows for local searches, above the regular blue links. For "near me" and "service + area" searches, being *in* that pack is often worth more than any other single thing — most clicks and calls go there. "You're not in the map pack for your own service" is one of the most powerful, easy-to-grasp lines you can hand an owner.
:::

## Turn findings into one compelling opener

You now have a checklist full of symptoms. Do **not** dump all of them on the prospect — that's overwhelming and reads as showing off. Pick the **single most revenue-relevant finding** and frame it as *observation → cost → curiosity*:

- **Observation (specific, true):** "I searched 'dentist in Jayanagar' and your clinic didn't come up in the top results, but [Competitor] did — and they've got 180 reviews to your 6."
- **Cost (their language, not jargon):** "That's the exact moment a nearby patient is choosing who to call — and right now that's going to them, not you."
- **Curiosity (a low-pressure open door):** "I fix this kind of thing for clinics. Happy to show you the two or three changes that'd move it — no charge to take a look. Worth a quick chat?"

No "SEO." No "algorithm." No acronyms. Just: here's what I saw, here's what it means for your patients, want me to help?

:::note[💰 Rupee check]
Illustrative — make the maths *their* maths. If you know a new patient/customer is worth, say, ₹5,000–₹20,000 to this business, translate the finding into that currency: "even a couple of extra patients a month from being visible again is worth far more than what I'd charge." You're not quoting a price yet — you're planting the idea that fixing this *makes* money, so your fee is an investment, not a cost. Keep numbers as ranges and clearly hypothetical until you've confirmed their real figures.
:::

:::tip[⚔️ War story]
A freelancer we'll call Aisha (composite) wanted to pitch a busy dental clinic near her home. Instead of emailing "I do SEO," she spent fifteen minutes on the pre-audit and found one thing: the clinic's Google profile was listed under the *wrong category* and didn't reply to a single review — including a nasty one sitting right at the top. She walked in at a quiet hour, showed the owner that one review on her phone, and said, "This is the first thing new patients read about you, and no one's answered it." That single specific, undeniable observation got her a fifteen-minute chat, which got her the account. She didn't pitch marketing. She pointed at a fire.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Dumping *all* your findings at once — pick the one that most obviously costs them customers.
- Using jargon (SEO, SERP, algorithm, backlinks) with an owner who just wants more calls.
- Auditing from your own logged-in browser and reporting flattering, personalised results.
- Skipping the pre-audit and cold-pitching generically "to save time" — that's the slow way, because it doesn't work.

## Do this now

Run the full 15-minute pre-audit on your **top prospect** from the last lesson (and one competitor). Fill in the checklist. Then write your **one-line opener** using the observation → cost → curiosity structure. That single sentence is what you'll actually say or send when you reach out. Save it — you'll use it in the Assignment.
