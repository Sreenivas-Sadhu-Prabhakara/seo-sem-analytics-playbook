The prospect said the magic words: "Send me something." This is where deals go to die.

Because the new freelancer either sends nothing (they get busy, the moment cools, ghosted) or sends a *monster* — a 12-page deck full of "leveraging omnichannel synergies" that the owner never opens. Either way, no signature.

Your job is to strike while it's warm with something short, clear, and impossible to misread: a **one-page proposal** and a **simple scope agreement.** That's it. Let's build both.

## Why one page wins

A local business owner is busy, non-technical, and allergic to fluff. A one-page proposal respects that. It also forces *you* to be clear — if you can't say the plan in a page, you don't have a plan, you have a vibe. Long proposals aren't more professional; they're more *ignored.*

## The one-page proposal structure

Six blocks, in this order, because it's the order the owner's brain wants:

1. **Their problem** — mirror back what you found in the pre-audit, in *their* language. "Your clinic doesn't appear for 'dentist in Jayanagar,' and competitors with more reviews are capturing those patients." This proves you listened and sets up everything below.
2. **Your plan** — the fix, in plain steps. "Claim and optimise your Google profile, build up genuine reviews, fix your site so it loads fast on phones and shows a call button." No jargon dumps.
3. **Deliverables** — the concrete things they *get*. Bullets. "Fully optimised Google Business Profile. A review-request system. 4 optimised service pages. A monthly report." Tangible beats abstract.
4. **Timeline** — realistic phases, not fantasy dates. "Month 1: foundations & profile. Months 2–3: reviews, content, rankings building." Bake in that SEO compounds over months.
5. **Price** — your tier (or the good-better-best table), stated plainly, with payment terms. No burying it.
6. **The next step** — one clear action. "Reply 'yes' or WhatsApp me and I'll send the agreement and start this week." Remove every ounce of friction.

:::note[📋 Swipe this]
**One-page proposal template** — fill the brackets:

> **Proposal for [Business name]**
> Prepared by [Your name] · [Date] · [Phone/WhatsApp]
>
> **What I noticed:** [The single biggest pre-audit finding, in their words — the cost to them.]
>
> **What I'll do:** [3–5 plain-English steps to fix it.]
>
> **What you'll get:**
> - [Deliverable 1]
> - [Deliverable 2]
> - [Deliverable 3]
> - [A monthly report so you see progress]
>
> **Timeline:** [Month 1…], [Months 2–3…]. *(Local SEO builds over months — this is steady, not overnight.)*
>
> **Investment:** [Chosen tier] — **₹[amount]/month** (or one-off ₹[amount]). [Payment terms: e.g. 50% to start, balance on [date]. Ad budget, if any, is separate and paid to Google directly.]
>
> **Next step:** Reply "yes" and I'll send a short scope agreement and begin by [date].

Keep it to one page. If it spills over, you're over-explaining — cut.
:::

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha — set the SEO-timeline expectation *in writing*]
The fastest way to lose a client in month two is to let them expect page-one rankings in week one. SEO compounds; it takes months. Say so *in the proposal and out loud*: "the profile and review wins show up fast; organic rankings build over 2–4 months." Under-promise the timeline, over-deliver the early quick wins (a fixed GBP, first new reviews). Managed expectations are retained clients.
:::

## The pitch that carries the proposal

The proposal is the document; the *pitch* is you delivering it — in person, on a call, or over WhatsApp. Keep it the same shape as the paper: **their problem → your plan → the value → the ask.** Lead with the pre-audit insight (you've already earned attention with it), walk them through the plan in their language, tie it to money ("a couple of extra patients covers the whole fee"), and then — this is the part beginners skip — **actually ask for the business.** "Shall we start this week?" Silence after the ask is fine. Let them answer. Whoever speaks first often loses; let it be them.

## The simple contract (yes, even for your uncle)

Here's where new freelancers get burned: they do the work on a handshake, then get scope-crept, paid late, or blamed for things they never agreed to do. A one-page **scope agreement** prevents 90% of that. It doesn't need a lawyer or legalese — it needs to be *clear and mutually signed* (even a WhatsApp "I agree" on a written summary is far better than nothing; for anything sizeable, a signed PDF).

Cover these five things:

- **Scope — what's included.** List the exact deliverables. Specific.
- **What's NOT included.** This one line saves your life. "Does not include: paid ad budget, website hosting fees, professional photography, social media posting." Name the obvious add-ons people *assume* are free.
- **Payment terms.** Amount, when it's due, and how (UPI/bank). State the advance.
- **Timeline & reporting.** When work starts, when they get a report.
- **Cancellation.** How either side ends it — e.g. "either party may cancel with 15 days' notice; work done is billable to that date."

:::note[📋 Swipe this — plain-English scope blurb]
> **Scope agreement — [Business] & [You]**
> **Included:** [list your exact deliverables]. **Reporting:** monthly, by [date].
> **Not included:** paid advertising budget (paid directly to Google), website hosting/domain fees, professional photo/video shoots, social-media content posting, and any work not listed above (quoted separately).
> **Payment:** ₹[amount], with **50% (₹___) to begin** and the balance by [date], via UPI/bank transfer. Monthly retainers are billed on the [1st] and due within [7] days.
> **Term & cancellation:** month-to-month; either side may cancel with **15 days' notice**; completed work is payable to the cancellation date.
> **Agreed:** [Your name] — [Client name] — [Date].
:::

:::note[💰 Rupee check]
Illustrative norm, verify locally: freelancers commonly take an **advance of ~50% upfront** (or the first month upfront for retainers) before starting, with the balance on delivery or by a set date. The advance isn't rudeness — it filters out non-serious clients, funds your work, and signals you're a professional, not a favour. New freelancers who skip the advance are the ones chasing payments for months. Ask for it every time.
:::

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha — scope creep is how you go broke while busy]
"While you're at it, can you also just…" is the most expensive sentence in freelancing. Design the logo. Post to Instagram daily. Fix the printer. Each "small" extra, unbilled, quietly turns your ₹20,000 retainer into ₹200/hour. Your defence is the *"Not included"* line: when a request lands outside scope, you don't refuse — you *re-quote.* "Happy to do that — it's outside our current scope, so I'll send a quick add-on quote." Friendly, firm, and paid. The scope doc is what lets you say it without a fight.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Sending a bloated multi-page deck instead of a crisp one-pager.
- Never actually *asking* for the business — presenting, then trailing off.
- Working on a pure handshake, then getting scope-crept and paid late.
- Not naming what's *excluded*, so the client assumes photography, ad budget, and social posting are all "part of it."
- Promising fast rankings and getting fired in month two for reality.

<details>
<summary>Q: Why does even a friend or relative's business need a written scope agreement?</summary>

Because friendship makes scope creep *worse*, not better — a friend feels freer to ask for "just one more thing," and money conversations with family are awkward enough without ambiguity. A short written scope (what's included, what's not, payment, cancellation) protects the relationship by making expectations explicit, so nobody feels taken advantage of. Clarity is kindness.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q: The client asks you to "also just handle our Instagram posts" mid-retainer. What's the professional response?</summary>

Don't silently absorb it and don't flatly refuse. Point to scope and re-quote: "Happy to help with that — it's outside our current scope, so I'll send a quick add-on quote." This keeps the client happy, protects your margins, and reinforces that your time has a price. The "Not included" line in your agreement is exactly what makes this easy.
</details>
