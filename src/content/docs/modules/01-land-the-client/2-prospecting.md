Open Google Maps. Search "dentist near me" — or whatever your niche is — in a neighbourhood you know. Look at the first ten results.

You'll see it within thirty seconds: the clinic with no photos. The one with a 3.2 rating and not a single reply to an angry review. The one whose "website" is a dead JustDial listing. The one that doesn't appear at all until you scroll past the ads.

Every one of those is a prospect. Not because you *hope* they need help — because you can *see* they do, from your sofa, for free. That's the whole game of prospecting: find businesses whose problems are visible from the outside, so your pitch is a diagnosis, not a guess.

## Why "visible problems" beats "who might need marketing"

Cold-pitching "hi, do you want more customers?" is begging. Everyone wants more customers; nobody wants a stranger's sales pitch. But if you can say, "I noticed your clinic doesn't show up when people search 'dentist in Jayanagar,' and your top competitor has 200 reviews to your 4" — now you're not selling, you're *reporting a fire.* People act on fires.

So we hunt for prospects by their *symptoms.* And local SMBs wear their symptoms in public.

## The weak-GBP goldmine

Your single richest vein is the weak **Google Business Profile (GBP)** — the free listing that decides whether a local business shows up on Maps and in the local pack. Most local SMBs have one, and most treat it like a chore they did once in 2019. Symptoms you can spot in minutes:

- **Unclaimed or thin profile** — "Claim this business" showing, or missing hours, category, phone, or address.
- **Few or no reviews**, or reviews with zero owner replies — a dead giveaway nobody's minding the shop.
- **No photos, or three dark phone snaps** from years ago.
- **No website**, or a link to a broken/abandoned page.
- **Wrong category** (a physio listed as "doctor," a cloud kitchen as "restaurant") quietly killing their relevance.
- **Barely ranks** — search their main "service + area" term and they're buried under competitors.

:::note[🎓 Foundations]
The **Google Business Profile** is the free listing that powers the map pin, the hours, the reviews, the "call/directions" buttons — the stuff that shows in the little box on Google and Maps. (It used to be managed by a "Google My Business" app; that brand is retired — it's now managed straight from Google Search and Maps.) For a local business, a weak GBP is like a shop with the shutters half down. Fixing it is often the fastest, most visible win you can deliver — which makes weak GBPs the perfect prospecting signal.
:::

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha — menus move]
Google reshuffles where you manage a profile constantly. If you're helping an owner and the settings aren't where you expect, search Google for "manage my business profile" while signed into their account rather than hunting pixel-by-pixel. Teach the *goal* (claim it, fix the category, add photos), not a click-path that'll be stale next quarter.
:::

## Where to look for prospects

You don't need fancy tools. You need to look where local demand already lives:

1. **Google Maps** — the motherlode. Search your niche + area, scan for the weak-symptom list above. Note the buried ones *and* the leaders (you'll use the leaders as "look what your competitor is doing" ammo).
2. **JustDial, Sulekha, IndiaMART** — businesses listed here are already paying *someone* for leads, which means they (a) value marketing and (b) have a budget. A JustDial listing with no proper website is a lovely prospect.
3. **Local Facebook & Instagram** — neighbourhood groups, "shop local [your city]" pages. Businesses posting into these care about growth but often have a messy Google presence.
4. **The high street you can literally walk.** Old-school and underrated. Walk your market road. The salon with the beautiful interiors and a nonexistent online presence is a warm walk-in waiting to happen — you've *been there*, so your outreach starts with genuine familiarity.

:::note[📋 Swipe this]
**Prospect-list template** — one row per business, in a plain spreadsheet:

| Business name | Niche | Area | Phone / contact | GBP status (claimed? #reviews / rating) | Website? (Y/N/broken) | Visible problem (1 line) | Warm intro? | Priority (H/M/L) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Fill 20–30 rows before you contact anyone. The "Visible problem" column is the seed of your pitch. The "Warm intro?" column is your unfair advantage — always sort those to the top.
:::

## Read the signals of ability *and* willingness to pay

A prospect with a problem you can fix is only half the equation. They also need to be able and willing to pay. Look for tells:

- **Already spending on leads** — a JustDial paid listing, boosted Instagram posts, a hoarding, a flyer. Someone who spends on marketing will spend on better marketing.
- **Decent premises / real staff** — a clinic with three chairs and two receptionists has cash flow; a one-person side hustle run from a phone often doesn't.
- **Repeat-value customers** — dentists, gyms, salons, coaching centres keep customers for months or years, so a new customer is worth real money to them, so your fee is easy maths.
- **Owner is reachable and decides fast** — a founder-run local business beats a chain where you'll die in "let me check with head office."

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha — don't chase businesses that can't pay you]
The tiny tea stall with a heartbreaking Google profile is *not* your client, however much you want to help. If a business's entire monthly profit is smaller than your fee, no amount of great work makes the maths work — you'll burn weeks, feel guilty charging, and quietly resent it. Kind rule of thumb: if one new customer isn't worth at least a few thousand rupees to them, keep walking. Qualify *for budget* before you fall in love with the fix.
:::

:::note[💰 Rupee check]
Illustrative, verify locally: a good early client is one where your monthly fee is a *small fraction* of the extra revenue you can plausibly generate. If a dentist earns, say, ₹5,000–₹30,000 from a single new patient's treatment, a retainer that brings even two extra patients a month pays for itself many times over — and that's the story you'll tell in the pitch. Match your fee to the client's economics, not to your rent panic.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Building a list of businesses you'd *like* to help instead of ones that show fixable symptoms and can pay.
- Ignoring the warm-intro column and cold-blasting strangers when a relative could open three doors.
- Falling for the saddest profile in town (heart-tugging, broke) over the boring profitable clinic that'll actually sign.
- Prospecting forever and pitching never. A list is not income.

## Do this now

For your chosen niche, build a **prospect list of at least 20 real local businesses** using the template above. Fill in the GBP status, whether they have a working website, and a one-line visible problem for each. Flag every warm intro. Sort by priority. In the next lesson you'll run a proper 15-minute pre-audit on your top candidate and turn it into a pitch.

<details>
<summary>Q: Why is a business already listed (and paying) on JustDial often a *better* prospect than one with no online presence at all?</summary>

Because it proves two things you can't easily verify otherwise: they value marketing enough to pay for it, and they have a budget. A total zero-presence business might be broke, tech-phobic, or indifferent. The JustDial spender has already decided marketing is worth money — you just have to show them a better return.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q: You find a salon with a gorgeous shopfront but a near-invisible Google profile and no website. What makes this a strong prospect specifically?</summary>

Visible fixable problem (weak GBP, no site) *plus* signals of ability to pay (real premises, appointment-based repeat customers, image-conscious owner). The gap between their nice physical presence and their poor online presence is exactly the "you're leaving money on the table" story that closes deals.
</details>
