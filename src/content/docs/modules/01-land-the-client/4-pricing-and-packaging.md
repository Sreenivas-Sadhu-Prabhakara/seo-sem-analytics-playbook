The prospect is warming up. They liked your insight. And then it comes: "So… how much?"

This is the moment most new freelancers fall apart. They mumble. They discount before anyone objects. They blurt a number that's either insultingly low or plucked from panic. Some quote so cheap they end up working for less than the clinic's receptionist earns.

Let's fix that. Pricing isn't a personality trait you're born without — it's a set of models and a bit of nerve. By the end of this lesson you'll have both.

## The four offers you'll actually sell

Almost everything you'll ever sell to a local SMB is one of these four. Know all four, because different clients want different shapes.

**1. The one-off Audit.** A paid, deep version of your pre-audit — a written report of what's broken and what to fix, sometimes with a call to walk them through it. Low commitment for the client, fast cash for you, and a brilliant *foot in the door*: fix-it work and retainers often follow the audit.

**2. The monthly retainer (SEO / local).** Ongoing work for a fixed monthly fee — GBP management, reviews, on-page fixes, content, local rankings, reporting. This is the goal (more on why below).

**3. Google Ads management.** You run their paid search/display campaigns. Charged as a **flat monthly fee**, or a **percentage of ad spend** (commonly quoted around 10–20% of spend, illustrative — verify), or a hybrid. Note: the ad budget goes to Google; *your fee is separate* — say that out loud so nobody thinks your fee includes their clicks.

**4. Project work.** Defined-scope, one-time jobs: a website fix, a landing page, a GBP overhaul, a tracking/GA4 setup. A price, a deliverable, an end date. Great for clients not ready to commit monthly.

:::note[🎓 Foundations]
**Retainer** = a fixed recurring fee for ongoing work (usually monthly). **Project** = one price for one defined job with an end. **% of spend** = your fee scales with how much the client spends on ads. You'll mix these: many clients start with an *audit or project*, then graduate to a *retainer* once they trust you.
:::

## Why the retainer is the promised land

Project work pays, but every project ends — and then you're hunting again, back to zero next month. Retainers give you **predictable, recurring income**: you stack a few and you can actually forecast rent. They also suit the work honestly, because **SEO is not a one-week miracle** — local rankings, reviews, and content compound over *months.* A retainer aligns the money with the reality. Your aim across this course is to convert audits and projects into retainers.

## Good-better-best: package, don't just price

Never present one price — present **three tiers.** It's the oldest trick in retail because it works: given one option, people decide *yes or no*; given three, they decide *which one.* You move the conversation from "should I?" to "which?"

| Tier | Rough contents (illustrative) | Who it's for |
|---|---|---|
| **Basic** | GBP optimisation + reviews + monthly report | Tiny business, testing the water |
| **Growth** | Everything in Basic + on-page SEO + content + local citations | The typical local SMB serious about leads |
| **Pro** | Everything in Growth + Google Ads management + landing pages + priority support | Established business ready to spend to grow |

Most clients pick the middle — which is exactly where you want your best-value offer to sit. The top tier makes the middle look sensible; the bottom tier catches the cautious.

:::note[📋 Swipe this]
When you present tiers, say: *"I've put together three options depending on how fast you want to move. Most clinics like yours start on Growth — it covers the visibility problem we talked about and it's the best value. Basic is if you want to start small, and Pro adds paid ads if you want leads faster."* Guide them to the middle without pushing. Naming *their* problem inside the pitch ("the visibility problem we talked about") ties the tier back to the pre-audit.
:::

## Value-based vs hourly (charge for the outcome)

Hourly billing punishes you for getting good: the faster and better you work, the *less* you earn. Worse, it invites clients to nickel-and-dime your minutes. Wherever you can, price the **outcome and the value**, not the clock. A GBP overhaul that brings a dentist ₹40,000 of new patients is worth the same whether it took you three hours or eight. Sell the result.

:::tip[🚀 Level up]
**Value-based pricing** anchors your fee to what the result is worth *to the client*, not what it costs *you*. Before quoting, ask questions that surface value: "What's a new patient worth to you over a year?" "How many more could you handle?" If a client can comfortably take 10 more customers a month at ₹8,000 each, a ₹15,000–₹25,000 retainer (illustrative) is trivially justified — and now you're discussing *return on investment*, not *cost.* This is an intermediate move; it takes nerve and good questions, but it's where real freelance income lives.
:::

## India rupee ranges (clearly illustrative)

:::note[💰 Rupee check]
**Illustrative only — the market moves, cities differ, and your experience changes everything. Verify current local rates before quoting; do not treat these as fixed.**

- **One-off audit:** roughly ₹3,000–₹15,000 depending on depth and whether a walkthrough call is included.
- **Local SEO retainer:** commonly ₹8,000–₹30,000+/month for an SMB; higher as your track record and the client's size grow.
- **Google Ads management:** a flat ₹8,000–₹25,000+/month, *or* ~10–20% of ad spend, *plus* the client's separate ad budget that goes to Google.
- **Project work:** priced per job — a landing page, a GBP overhaul, a GA4/tracking setup — from a few thousand rupees up, by scope.

These are ballparks to calibrate against, not a rate card. Price to the client's economics and your confidence, and revisit as you grow.
:::

## Handling "that's too expensive"

It's not an insult; it's the start of a negotiation, and often just a reflex. Don't crumble and don't slash your price on the spot. Instead:

- **Reframe to value:** "I hear you. Let's look at it against what one extra patient a month is worth to you — the fee's a fraction of that."
- **Trade scope, not rate:** if they truly can't stretch, drop them to a *smaller tier* (Basic instead of Growth) rather than doing Growth for a Basic price. Less work for less money keeps your rate intact.
- **Offer a paid audit as an on-ramp:** "Let's start with the audit so you see the value before committing monthly."
- **Be willing to walk.** A client who only ever competes on price will bleed you dry. Politely leaving is a valid, and sometimes profitable, answer.

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha — don't underprice your first client to zero]
"I'll do it free/almost-free to get started" is a fine *strategy* — for **one** client, deliberately, in exchange for a testimonial and a case study, with a clear end date or "this is a special launch rate." It becomes a *trap* when it's your reflex for every client, or when "free trial" quietly becomes "free forever." Even a case-study client should sign a scope and, ideally, pay *something* — people value what they pay for, and free clients are famously the most demanding. Discount the *price*, never the *professionalism*.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Quoting one price instead of three tiers, turning "which?" back into "yes or no?"
- Charging hourly and capping your own income the moment you get efficient.
- Folding on price the instant someone flinches, instead of reframing to value or trading scope.
- Forgetting to state that the **ad budget is separate** from your Ads-management fee.
- Free-forever "trials" that never convert.

## Do this now

Build your **good-better-best menu** for your niche: three tiers, rough contents for each, and an illustrative monthly figure you'd be willing to say out loud. Then write one sentence answering "that's too expensive" in your own words. You'll fold real numbers into the proposal in the next lesson.
