Two physiotherapy websites. Same city, same services, similar prices.

Site A: a real clinic name, photos of the actual team, the lead physio's name and qualifications, fifteen years mentioned, an address with a map, forty Google reviews embedded, clear phone and hours. Site B: "PhysioCare Solutions," stock photo of a smiling stranger, no names, no address, no faces, a contact form and nothing else. You're the patient with a bad back. Which one do you call?

You didn't even hesitate. Neither does Google. That instinct — *this business is real, expert, and safe to trust* — has a name in SEO: **E-E-A-T.** This lesson is about earning it, credibly, for a small local business.

## Why this pays

Trust is the invisible conversion multiplier. Two pages can have identical on-page SEO, but the one that *feels* trustworthy gets the call. And in trust-sensitive niches — clinics, dentists, financial advisors, lawyers — trust isn't a nice-to-have, it's the whole ballgame. Clients here will pay you specifically to make their site *feel* as credible as they actually are. Half of them are genuinely excellent and terrible at showing it. That gap is your fee.

## What E-E-A-T actually is

:::note[🎓 Foundations]
**E-E-A-T = Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trust.** It comes from the guidelines Google gives its human quality raters — the people who evaluate whether search results are good. Crucially: **E-E-A-T is not a dial in a dashboard you can turn up.** There's no "E-E-A-T score" you edit. It's a *quality framework* describing what trustworthy content looks like, which Google's systems try to approximate. So you don't "do E-E-A-T" as a checkbox — you make the business genuinely, visibly trustworthy, and the signals follow.

The four, in plain terms for a local business:

- **Experience** — has the author/business actually *done* this? (Treated real patients, run the shop for years.)
- **Expertise** — do they have the knowledge/qualifications? (Degrees, certifications, training.)
- **Authoritativeness** — are they known and cited as a go-to? (Reviews, mentions, reputation.)
- **Trust** — the big one, the centre of it all — is the site safe, honest and reliable? (Real contact info, accurate claims, secure site, no dark patterns.)
:::

The extra "E" — **Experience** — is the one small businesses win on easily and forget to show. A physio who's treated a thousand knees has experience a slick faceless content-farm never will. Your job is to make that experience *visible on the page.*

## Practical E-E-A-T signals for an SMB

You can't hand Google an "authority" file. You *can* build the real-world signals it looks for. Here's the practical checklist for a local business site.

**Real people — bios, faces, credentials.**
Put the humans on the site. A named lead practitioner with a photo and real qualifications ("Priya Nair, BPT, MPT (Sports), 12 years' experience") does more for trust than any keyword. Author bios on articles; a team page with real faces. In YMYL niches, anonymity is a trust-killer.

**Real photos.**
Actual clinic, actual team, actual treatment (with consent). Stock photos of gleaming foreign clinics say "we're hiding something." Real, slightly-imperfect photos of the actual Indiranagar reception say "we're right here, come in."

**NAP, address and a map.**
Name, Address, Phone — consistent everywhere, matching the Google Business Profile — plus an embedded map. A verifiable physical location is a foundational trust signal; a site with no address is a site with something to hide.

**Reviews on the site, not just on Maps.**
Embed or quote real Google reviews (ideally naming the service). Reputation *off* the site feeds authority; showing it *on* the site closes the visitor.

**Clear contact info and policies.**
Phone, WhatsApp, email, hours, and where relevant honest policies — cancellation, what to expect, pricing approach. Transparency reads as trust.

**HTTPS and a working, professional site.**
A secure padlock is table stakes — a "Not secure" browser warning is a trust catastrophe, as is a broken, typo-riddled site. Basic professionalism *is* a trust signal.

**Accurate, honest, current information.**
Right hours, prices/ranges and services; no expired offers, no invented claims. Inaccuracy quietly erodes trust with Google and humans alike.

## Why trust matters double for health and money

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
**Anonymous, faceless sites in trust-sensitive niches are fighting uphill — and deserve to be.** Google's rater guidelines apply an especially high bar to **YMYL** topics — *Your Money or Your Life* — the pages that can affect someone's health, finances, safety or major life decisions: clinics, hospitals, financial advice, legal, "is this supplement safe," "how to invest my savings." On these, unknown authorship, no credentials, no real business identity, and thin or inaccurate content are treated as genuinely risky, because a wrong answer can hurt someone.

So a physio, dental, finance or legal client with a faceless site isn't just missing SEO points — they're in the category Google scrutinises hardest, showing none of the signals it most wants. **For these clients, adding real names, credentials and a verifiable identity isn't polish. It's the priority.**
:::

The flip side is the opportunity: your genuinely-qualified local client can *out-trust* the content farms effortlessly, because they have the one thing the farms fake and never have — real experience and a real, accountable identity. You just have to put it on the page.

:::tip[🚀 Level up]
**Reviewed-by and expert citations.** For YMYL articles, add a "Medically reviewed by Dr. [Name], [credentials]" line linking to that person's real bio — and make sure it's true (they actually reviewed it). Where the client is genuinely quoted or featured (a local paper, an industry body, an association listing), reference it. Keep author bios consistent across the site. These are the durable, honest ways to build authority — slow, real and unfakeable, which is exactly why they work.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Faceless site in a YMYL niche** — no names, no credentials, no address. Fix first.
- **Stock photos only.** Instant "who are these people, really?"
- **Reviews stranded on Maps**, never surfaced on the site.
- **Fake or borrowed credentials.** Never. Inventing qualifications is fraud, not SEO — and it's the opposite of trust.
- **"Not secure" browser warning** because the site isn't on HTTPS.
