Meena runs a dental clinic in Jayanagar, Bengaluru. (Meet **Meena's Dental Clinic** — your composite capstone client for this course; she's a hypothetical stitched from a dozen real ones.) In your kickoff call she says, proudly, "We should rank for *teeth*." You nod, sip your chai, and quietly make a note: *nobody in the history of Google has typed "teeth" wanting a root canal.*

That gap — between the words a business owner *thinks* customers use and the words they *actually* type at 11pm with a throbbing molar — is the entire job of keyword research. And you can do the whole thing for free.

## Why this pays

Every rupee you'll later spend on content, on-page work, or ads gets aimed at keywords. Aim at the wrong words and you'll rank #1 for phrases that bring zero patients. Nail the research and everything downstream compounds. This is the cheapest, highest-leverage hour in the whole engagement — which is exactly why sloppy freelancers skip it.

## Step 1 — Harvest seed keywords

Seeds are the obvious root phrases you build everything else from. You already have most of them:

- **From the business:** services, products, brand terms. For Meena: *dentist, dental clinic, root canal, teeth cleaning, braces, tooth extraction, dental implant.*
- **From the audit:** the pages that already exist, the services page headings, the words the owner and their reviews use.
- **From the customer's mouth:** how do walk-ins describe their problem? "Tooth pain," not "endodontic emergency." Real people use plain, sometimes wrong, words. Match them.

Write 10–20 seeds in a spreadsheet. Don't overthink it — seeds are just the door you walk through.

:::note[🎓 Foundations]
**What is "search volume"?** It's roughly how many times people search a term in a given area per month — an *estimate*, usually shown as a range (e.g. 1K–10K), not a precise count. It tells you demand exists; it says nothing about whether those searchers will ever buy. High volume + wrong intent = busy road to nowhere.
:::

## Step 2 — Google Keyword Planner (free, but you need a Google Ads account)

Keyword Planner lives inside **Google Ads**. You'll create a free Ads account to reach it — you do *not* have to run a single ad. (Tip: when Ads pushes you to launch a campaign during signup, look for the "switch to Expert Mode / create an account without a campaign" escape hatch — menus move, so if it's hidden, search Ads help for "create account without campaign.")

Inside, use **Tools → Keyword Planner → Discover new keywords**. Feed it your seeds and Meena's city. You'll get:

- **Keyword ideas** you'd never have guessed ("wisdom tooth removal cost," "invisible braces near me").
- **Average monthly searches** as ranges — set the location to India / Bengaluru so the numbers mean something.
- A rough **competition** signal (this is *ad* competition, a loose proxy for organic difficulty — don't treat it as gospel).

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
Keyword Planner shows *broad volume ranges* (like "1K–10K") to accounts that aren't spending money. That's fine — you want relative demand and idea discovery, not decimal precision. Anyone selling you "exact search volumes" is selling you false confidence.
:::

## Step 3 — Mine Google itself, for free

Google is the world's biggest keyword tool and it's sitting right there:

- **Autocomplete:** start typing "dentist in jayan…" and watch Google finish the thought with what real people search. Try "dentist near," "root canal," "braces cost."
- **People also ask:** the expandable question boxes on the results page — a goldmine of the exact questions customers have.
- **Related searches:** scroll to the bottom of the results page for eight more phrasings.

> 📸 *[Screenshot placeholder: Google autocomplete dropdown for "dentist in jayanagar" showing suggested completions]*

Do this for each seed and paste the good ones into your sheet. Fifteen minutes here beats any paid tool for local nuance.

## Step 4 — Google Trends for seasonality and geography

[Google Trends](https://trends.google.com) is free and answers two questions paid tools fumble:

- **Seasonality:** does demand spike? "Gym near me" surges every January; "tax filing" peaks before July. If Meena adds teeth-whitening, is there a wedding-season bump? Plan content and ad budget around the wave, not against it.
- **Geography:** compare two phrasings and see which region prefers which. Useful when you're unsure whether locals say "dental clinic" or "dental hospital" (in much of India, "hospital" is used far more loosely than you'd expect).

Trends shows *relative* interest over time, not absolute volume — use it for shape and timing, pair it with Planner for scale.

## Step 5 — Organise into themes

A flat list of 200 keywords is noise. Group them into **themes** — clusters that map to one page or one campaign:

| Theme | Example keywords | Likely target |
|---|---|---|
| Root canal | root canal cost, root canal treatment jayanagar, rct near me | Service page |
| Braces / aligners | braces cost, invisible braces, clear aligners bangalore | Service page |
| Emergency | tooth pain relief, emergency dentist near me, broken tooth | GBP + service page |
| Cleaning / whitening | teeth cleaning, scaling cost, teeth whitening | Service page |

Themes are how a keyword list becomes a site plan. You'll formalise this into a keyword *map* in the Assignment.

:::note[📋 Swipe this]
**The 5-step free keyword-research process:**
1. Dump 10–20 **seeds** from the business + audit + how customers actually talk.
2. Expand in **Keyword Planner** (India location) for ideas + volume ranges.
3. Mine **autocomplete + People also ask + related searches** for real phrasing.
4. Check **Google Trends** for seasonality + which phrasing a region prefers.
5. **Cluster into themes**, each mapped to one page or campaign.
Total tool cost: ₹0.
:::

## Volume vs difficulty vs intent — you can't have all three

Every keyword sits on a triangle: **high volume, low difficulty, high buyer intent.** You almost never get all three — a term with big volume, easy competition, *and* ready-to-buy searchers doesn't stay unclaimed for long.

- **High volume + low intent** = "what is a root canal" (traffic, few patients).
- **High intent + high difficulty** = "root canal cost bangalore" (worth it, but you'll fight for it).
- **The sweet spot for SMBs** = the **long tail**: longer, specific, lower-volume phrases like "painless root canal jayanagar 4th block." Fewer searches each, but far less competition, far higher intent, and dozens of them add up. A dozen long-tail wins beats one impossible head-term dream.

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
**Chasing vanity keywords.** New freelancers fall in love with the fat head term — "dentist" — because the volume number is thrillingly large. But it's brutally competitive, drowning in national chains, and half the searchers aren't even in Bengaluru. You'll spend six months and rank on page 4. Meanwhile a dozen long-tail terms with real intent were sitting there, winnable in weeks. Volume is a vanity metric until intent and difficulty are in the room.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Researching in your own head instead of in the tools — you are not your client's customer.
- Ignoring long-tail phrases because the volume looks "too small" to bother with.
- Treating Keyword Planner's competition column as *organic* difficulty (it's an ad metric).
- Building a giant flat list and never grouping it into themes.

## Do this now

Open a spreadsheet for your capstone client. Write 15 seed keywords. Run three of them through Keyword Planner (create the free Ads account if you haven't), then mine autocomplete and "People also ask" for each. Paste every keyword worth keeping into the sheet and start tagging them into 4–6 themes. You've just started your keyword map.

<summary>Q: Why is the long tail so valuable for a small local business specifically?</summary>

Because SMBs can't win the big head terms against national competitors, but long-tail phrases are lower-competition, higher-intent, and hyper-specific to what the business actually does — so they're winnable quickly and the searchers are closer to buying. Many small wins beat one unattainable big one.
</details>
