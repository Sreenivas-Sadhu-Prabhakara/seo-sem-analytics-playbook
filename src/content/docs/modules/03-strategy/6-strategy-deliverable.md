You've done the real thinking — keywords, intent, local nuance, priorities, honest goals. It's all in your head and scattered across three spreadsheet tabs. Now comes the moment that separates the freelancer who gets paid from the one who "did a lot of good work, apparently."

You have to put it on paper. One document. Skimmable enough that a busy dentist between patients reads it in ten minutes and says "yes, do this." That document is your **strategy and proposal** — and it's the first thing you can genuinely charge for, before a single title tag is touched.

## Why this pays

Two reasons. First, the strategy doc is a **paid deliverable in its own right** — many freelancers give it away to win the "real" work; the sharp ones sell the strategy as a low-risk first engagement that builds trust and often converts into the retainer. Second, the doc is your **contract of scope and expectations.** When the owner signs it, you both agreed what you're doing, in what order, for what money, aiming at what outcome. No sign-off, no shield — and no surprises three months in.

## The strategy-doc structure

Keep it tight and in this order — each section answers the owner's silent question:

1. **Executive summary / goals** *(what are we trying to achieve?)* — 3–4 lines: the honest, lead-tied goal, the timeframe, and the headline of how you'll get there. It's the only part some owners fully read, so make it count.
2. **Where you are today (baseline)** *(what's the starting point?)* — a short snapshot from the audit + Module 4 baseline: current visibility, GBP state, key problems.
3. **Target keywords & themes** *(who are we trying to reach?)* — not a dump of 300 phrases. Show the **themes**, priority terms, and intent — a compact table or the keyword map itself. Proves you understand their customers.
4. **The channel plan** *(what will we actually do?)* — grouped into **Local SEO / GBP**, **On-page / website**, and **Google Ads**, each with 2–3 concrete moves and the *business reason*. Not "optimise meta descriptions" — "rewrite the five service-page titles so you show up for the treatments people search."
5. **The 90-day roadmap** *(in what order, and when?)* — your prioritisation timeline made visible: quick wins in weeks 1–2, lead engines in weeks 3–6, compounding work in weeks 7–12. A simple table beats a fancy Gantt chart.
6. **Measurement plan** *(how will we know it's working?)* — the leading and lagging metrics you'll track, reported monthly. Reassures the owner they won't be flying blind.
7. **Investment** *(what does it cost?)* — your fee, and (if running ads) the recommended ad spend, clearly separated. Tie it to the goal so the number reads as an investment, not an expense.
8. **The ask / next step** *(what do you need from me?)* — an explicit "approve this to begin" line, and the access you need (GBP, etc.).

## Keep it skimmable — the owner is not reading a thesis

Your client runs a business between putting out fires. A 40-page report is not thorough; it's *ignored*. Write for a skimmer:

- **Lead with outcomes, not tactics.** Headings and bullets say what the owner *gets*; the tactic is the supporting detail.
- **Tables over paragraphs** for keywords and the roadmap.
- **Bold the numbers and the promises.** The goal, the timeframe, the investment.
- **Plain language.** If a line contains "leverage," "synergy," or "holistic omnichannel," delete it. The owner doesn't want to feel stupid; they want to feel confident.
- **One page of substance beats ten of padding.** Depth lives in your spreadsheets; the doc is the decision-making summary.

## Tie every action to a business outcome

This is the difference between a proposal that closes and a to-do list that confuses. For every single line, answer the owner's real question — *"what's in it for my business?"*

| ❌ Tactic-speak | ✅ Outcome-speak |
|---|---|
| "Optimise GBP categories and attributes" | "Get you into the Maps '3-pack' so nearby patients find and call you" |
| "Fix H1s and title tags on service pages" | "Show up for 'root canal jayanagar' and turn more clicks into bookings" |
| "Run search ads on 2 keywords" | "Start getting enquiries *this month* while the free listings build up" |
| "Implement review-generation workflow" | "Turn happy patients into the reviews that win you the click over competitors" |

The owner doesn't buy "meta descriptions." They buy *more patients.* Speak their language.

:::tip[⚔️ War story]
A freelancer (composite) sent a boutique owner a genuinely brilliant 22-page strategy — audit tables, a keyword matrix, schema recommendations, the lot. On the merits, excellent. The owner opened it, saw a wall of jargon, thought *"I have no idea what any of this will do for me,"* and quietly ghosted. A week later she signed with someone whose "strategy" was a clean two-pager: three goals, a simple roadmap table, a clear price, one sentence per action explaining the *business* benefit. The best strategy the owner doesn't understand loses to a decent one the owner *gets*. Clarity closes.
:::

## Get explicit approval before you execute

Here's the rule that saves you from unpaid rework and awkward arguments: **do not start executing until the owner has explicitly approved the plan.** Not "seemed happy on the call." Explicitly — a reply, a signature, a WhatsApp "yes go ahead," anything you can point to.

Why it matters:

- It confirms **scope**, so "I thought that was included" never happens.
- It confirms the **budget and investment**.
- It confirms the **goals and timeline**, so month one doesn't get judged against a fantasy.
- It gives *them* ownership of the plan, which makes them a partner instead of a critic.

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
**Executing before the client agreed the plan** is how freelancers end up doing free work — or the *wrong* work. You enthusiastically rebuild five pages, then the owner says "oh, I wanted to focus on the new branch, not those services." Now you've burned hours you can't bill and shaken their confidence. Always close the strategy with a clear "approve this to begin," and *wait for the yes.* Enthusiasm is not authorisation.
:::

## Common mistakes

- A 40-page report nobody reads instead of a skimmable decision doc.
- Listing tactics without tying each to a business outcome.
- Burying the goal, timeline and price where the skimmer won't find them.
- Starting work on a "verbal maybe" instead of an explicit approval.

A clear plan the owner *gets* beats a technically superior one they don't.

</details>

Because the client is a busy owner who skims, not a marketer who studies documents. A skimmable doc that leads with outcomes, uses tables, and bolds the goal, timeline and price gets read and approved; a jargon-heavy report gets ignored. Depth belongs in your working spreadsheets — the doc is the decision-making summary.
