Two salons sit on the same street in Koramangala. Same prices, same haircuts, both a two-minute walk away. One has 34 reviews at 4.7 stars. The other has 6 reviews at 4.1. Which one gets the tap? You already know. And so does Google — which is why the first salon also *ranks* above the second.

Reviews are the closest thing local SEO has to a cheat code that isn't cheating. They pull the **prominence** lever harder than almost anything else, and at the exact same time they're the single biggest thing that converts a searcher into a walk-in. Ranking and conversion, one lever. If you build just *one* system for a local client, build a review engine.

## Why reviews do double duty

- **Ranking (prominence).** Volume, ratings, freshness and even the *words* in reviews all signal to Google that a business is trusted and real. A steady flow of recent, genuine reviews is one of the strongest prominence signals an SMB can generate.
- **Conversion (trust).** Star ratings are the first thing a human reads in the pack. More reviews and a higher average mean more taps, more calls, more bookings — often the difference between two otherwise identical shops.
- **Freshness matters.** A business with 200 reviews where the last one was 14 months ago looks stale. Ten reviews in the last month looks alive. Which brings us to velocity.

## Review velocity: a steady drip beats a flood

**Review velocity** is the rate at which new reviews arrive over time. A slow, natural, ongoing stream is what you want — and what looks legitimate.

The trap beginners fall into: they get the client excited, the client messages 40 customers in one afternoon, 25 reviews land in two days after months of silence, and the pattern looks unnatural. Google can filter or discount a sudden spike, and worse, a spike-then-silence pattern is exactly what fake-review farms produce. **Aim for a consistent trickle** — a handful of genuine reviews every week, forever — not a one-time blast. That means a *system*, not a campaign.

## The system: earn reviews ethically

Here's the whole play. Note the word **earn** — you're making it easy for happy customers to say what they already feel, never paying or faking.

### 1. Ask at the happy moment

Timing is everything. Ask when the customer is at peak happiness: right after the dentist says "all done, no cavities," right after the great haircut, when the delivery arrives hot, when the coaching-centre student clears the exam. A request at the moment of delight converts; a request three weeks later goes ignored.

### 2. Make it one tap — the short review link

Nobody will search Maps, find the business, scroll, and hunt for the review button. Remove every step. Google Business Profile gives you a **direct "ask for reviews" short link** (found in the profile's management view) that opens the review box straight away. Grab it, shorten it if you like, and turn it into:

- A **QR code** on the reception desk, the bill, the table tent, the delivery bag sticker.
- A **link** you paste into WhatsApp and SMS.
- A line in the email footer or the appointment-confirmation message.

> 📸 *[Screenshot placeholder: Google Business Profile "Ask for reviews" / "Get more reviews" panel showing the short review link and the option to share it]*

### 3. Follow up on WhatsApp / SMS

This is India — WhatsApp is where your customers live. A short, warm, personal message an hour or a day after the visit, with the one-tap link, is the workhorse of the whole system.

:::note[📋 Swipe this]
**Review-request WhatsApp / SMS script** (adapt the voice to the business):

> Hi [Name], thanks for visiting [Business] today — hope you're happy with [the service]! 🙏 If you have 30 seconds, a quick Google review really helps our small clinic. Just tap here: [short review link]. Thank you so much!

Keep it short, name the person, name the service, thank them, one link. For a follow-up nudge a couple of days later (only once — never nag):

> Hi [Name], just a gentle reminder — if you enjoyed your visit, we'd be so grateful for a quick review: [short review link]. No worries if you're busy! 😊

**The short-link trick:** get the "ask for reviews" link from the profile, run it through any URL shortener so it's clean and trackable, generate a free QR code from that short URL, and print it on the bill and reception desk. One asset, used everywhere.
:::

:::caution[⚠️ Gotcha]
**Do not filter or gate reviews.** "Text us privately if you're unhappy, but leave a Google review if you're happy" (review gating — routing bad experiences away from Google while funneling good ones in) is against Google's policy and can get your reviews removed. Ask *everyone* the same way. Yes, you'll get the occasional lower rating. A profile with a few 3-stars among the 5-stars is more believable than a suspicious wall of perfect scores — real businesses aren't flawless, and customers trust the ones that look real.
:::

## Respond to every review — good and bad

Responding isn't optional politeness; it's a signal of an engaged business and it visibly shapes how the *next* reader judges you. Reply to all of them.

**Positive reviews** — quick, warm, specific, not copy-paste:

> Thank you, Priya! So glad the root canal was comfortable — see you at your next check-up. 😊

**Negative reviews** — this is where you earn your fee. The reply is not for the angry customer; it's for the **hundred future customers reading it.** Stay calm, professional, and human:

1. **Thank them and acknowledge**, don't get defensive.
2. **Apologise for the experience** (not necessarily fault) and show you take it seriously.
3. **Take it offline** — give a number/email to resolve it directly.
4. **Keep it short and never argue** publicly. No excuses, no blaming the customer, no essay.

:::note[📋 Swipe this]
**Negative-review response template:**

> Hi [Name], I'm sorry your experience wasn't up to the mark — that's not the standard we hold ourselves to. We'd genuinely like to make it right. Could you call us on [number] or WhatsApp [number] so we can look into this personally? — [Owner name], [Business]

Never post private details, never argue the facts line-by-line, never accuse them of being a competitor even if you suspect it. A calm reply to a nasty review often impresses readers *more* than a wall of five stars.
:::

:::tip[🚀 Level up]
Watch for clearly **fake or extortion reviews** (a one-star from someone who was never a customer, or "pay me to remove this"). You can't always get them deleted, but you can **flag/report** them to Google for policy violation, and you should reply calmly for the record: "We have no record of your visit — please contact us so we can help." Don't let one fake review bait the owner into an angry public meltdown; that does more damage than the review.
:::

## The one line that can end your career: don't buy fake reviews

Let's be blunt. **Fake, paid, or incentivised reviews are a hard no.**

- They **violate Google's policies** — detected clusters get removed, and repeat offenders get profiles suspended, torching real reviews with them.
- In India they're increasingly a **legal and regulatory** problem — fake reviews and undisclosed paid endorsements sit squarely in the sights of consumer-protection rules. This is not a grey area you want your client, or you, standing in.
- They're **reputational suicide.** Customers spot the pattern (ten five-star reviews in one day, all generic, all new accounts), competitors report them, and the trust you were trying to build evaporates.

Offering a discount "in exchange for a positive review" is also out — that's an **incentivised** review and it's against the rules. You may thank customers and make reviewing effortless; you may not pay for the opinion or its star count. The entire value of this course is that it's honest. This is the line. Don't cross it, and don't work with a client who insists you do.

:::note[💰 Rupee check]
Review generation is usually folded into a **monthly local SEO retainer** rather than billed alone — but "we run your review system" is a big part of what justifies that retainer (often **₹8,000–₹30,000+/month**, illustrative and varying by city and scope). Never quote "we'll get you X reviews for ₹Y" as a purchase of reviews — you're selling a *system and the labour*, not the reviews themselves. Verify current market rates before you price.
:::

## Common mistakes

- One giant review blast, then silence (unnatural velocity).
- Gating reviews (funneling unhappy customers away from Google).
- Ignoring negative reviews, or worse, arguing with them publicly.
- Copy-paste "Thank you" on every positive review (looks robotic).
- Ever, under any pressure, buying or incentivising reviews.
